# Evidence against and open controversies

This file gathers the evidence against the presentation rules: null results, disputed studies, and advice that a study contradicts, by theme.

## Against assertion-evidence

- One experiment carries most of the weight: Garner 2013 (n = 110, one topic, one 8-minute talk, engineering students).
- The coauthor Alley is the main designer and promoter of the approach; the owner's site describes it as making talks more understandable (Leonhard Center 2026).
- The arms differ in words per slide (21.2 against 41.5) and in graphics; the design is a package, so the cause is unknown.
- AE viewers rated their slides as having a less appropriate amount of text than the other group did (Garner 2013).
- Immediate fact recall did not differ; the gain was in explanation and delayed items.
- Later studies measured other things: slide makers' workload (Alzayed 2023), grammar scores (Sripradith 2023), and pilots (Wolfe 2023).

## Against the principles as rules for talks

- Most principle studies are short narrated lessons on a computer; a live talk adds a person, a room and questions.
- Redundancy effects depend on prior knowledge and picture content (Adesope 2012); "never put text on the slide" overreaches.
- The modality effect can reverse under self-pacing (secondary); a talk is system-paced, so that result fits it better than it fits a page.
- Segmenting helped more for high prior knowledge in one account (secondary).
- Seductive details hurt (Sundararajan 2020), yet Wolfe 2023 reports that organiser graphics such as SmartArt can backfire, which supports testing images with an audience.

## Against the critics of slides

- Tufte 2003 argues the tool is the fault; his case is an essay with an accident report as its example, not a trial.
- CAIB 2003 is an investigation of an organisation; slides were one of several factors in what it found. The page numbers 191 and 201 came from a secondary page.
- Baker 2018 shows no average harm either, so "slides are bad" is not supported.
- Kosslyn 2012 shows violations are common, not that fixing them raises learning.

## Against the attention rule

- The belief that attention drops after 10 to 15 minutes has little primary support (Wilson 2007, Bradbury 2016).
- Short fixed formats (Ignite, five minutes) cannot cite it as evidence.

## Against the speaker-effect claims

- A visible face has no consistent effect over 12 studies (Henderson 2021).
- Enthusiasm raised engagement but not quiz scores (Marty-Dugas 2024).
- Agent studies use animated agents, not live speakers (Castro-Alonso 2021).

## Replications and notes

- Laptop against longhand notes: the quiz difference did not replicate in a direct replication (Urry 2019).
- Handout studies are not randomised (Worthington 2015).
- Practice pages report their own success: Designer, Ignite, PechaKucha, the memo, the assertion-evidence site.
