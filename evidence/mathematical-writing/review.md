## 1. Style guides: what mathematicians advise

| Source | Advice on structure | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Halmos 1970 | Organisation is a tree, almost never a chain; motivation first, then the statement, then the proof | convention, untested |
| Halmos 1970 | A theorem is one short sentence; a proof repeated twice signals a missing lemma | convention, untested |
| Knuth 1989 | A theorem statement stands alone, without leaning on assumptions in the preceding text | convention, untested |
| Knuth 1989 | Proofs carry signposts, such as "we divide the proof into two parts" | convention, untested |
| Krantz 2017 | Section 1 summarises the main results in plain words, then history, then the paper's plan | convention, untested |
| Krantz 2017 | Section 2 gives definitions and formal statements; Section 3 the big steps; lemma details go last | convention, untested |
| Krantz 2017 | Rule of thumb: of readers who read the abstract, 20% read the introduction and a quarter of those the body | convention, untested |
| Higham 2020 | Plan the high-level organisation early and rank the contributions (secondary, read in a summary of the 2nd edition) | convention, untested |
| Tao intro | The introduction states the main results, or an informal version labelled as such, compares with prior work, and outlines the proof | convention, untested |
| Tao organise | Milestones are self-contained propositions stated early; each lemma sits near its use | convention, untested |
| Tao organise | Draw a box per lemma and an arrow per dependency to find the sections | convention, untested |
| Tao errors | A paper split into lemmas survives one failed lemma; a global error is found by skimming the structure | convention, untested |
| Serre 2003 | The talk targets local language faults (articles, unnamed maps), not section structure (secondary) | convention, untested |
| Gillman 1987 | Listed by Knuth 1989 as a standard manual; not read for this review | not graded |

The guides disagree among themselves: Halmos 1970 quotes one adviser who praises chains of lemmas and another who finds them boring.

## 2. The template: genre analysis and publisher rules

| Source | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Graves 2013 | 30 pure and applied articles follow Introduction-Results, with no Methods or Discussion section | measured, descriptive |
| Kuteeva & McGrath 2015 | 22 pure articles plus author interviews: three compulsory stages, Openings, Proofs, Endings, and an optional post-introduction | measured, descriptive |
| Kuteeva & McGrath 2015 | All but one of the 22 announce the principal findings in the opening | measured, descriptive |
| Kuteeva & McGrath 2015 | 17 of 22 have a separate background or notation section; 11 of 22 have no conclusion | measured, descriptive |
| Kuteeva & McGrath 2015 | Some form of Move 2 (niche) appears in 10 of 22 introductions: 4 raise a question, 6 state a gap (sum of two counts) | measured, descriptive |
| Graves 2014 | Introductions establish presumptions such as definitions before results (secondary) | supported with caveat |
| AMS 2017 | AMS journals require an abstract without citations; the AMS proceedings checklist suggests at most about 150 words for short papers and 300 for long ones | convention; no citations enforced by the publisher, length only suggested |
| AMS 2017 | At least one primary MSC code is required; keywords are optional; theorem styles are plain, definition, remark | convention, enforced by the publisher |
| arXiv prep | Abstracts over 1920 characters are refused; mathematics has an optional MSC-class field | convention, enforced by the server |

## 4. Structured and calculational proofs

| Source | Claim | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Leron 1983 | Seminal: arrange a proof in levels, main ideas on top and details below, to communicate better | convention, untested until Fuller 2014 |
| Lamport 1995 | Numbered hierarchical steps, each with its own proof, aimed at rigor rather than communication | convention, untested |
| Lamport 1995 | Rewriting conventional proofs in this form found serious errors in almost all of one co-authored paper's proofs | supported with caveat (anecdote) |
| Lamport 2012 | Readability and rigor are separate goals; about 4 to 10 steps per level reads best; step names past level 4 are hard to read | convention, untested |
| Lamport 2012 | A proof sketch may sit before any level, so a reader can stop at the sketch | convention, untested |
| Dijkstra & Scholten 1990 | The calculational format: expression, relation, hint in braces, expression | convention, untested |

## 5. Empirical work on reading proofs

| Source | Design and result | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Mejia-Ramos 2012 | Seven-dimension model: terms, logical status, justifications, high-level idea, modular structure, transfer, examples | supported with caveat (model, built from interviews and surveys) |
| Mejia-Ramos 2017 | Method to build short, reliable multiple-choice comprehension tests per proof | measured |
| Inglis & Alcock 2012 | Eye tracking, 18 students and 12 mathematicians (secondary count): students dwell on formulae; experts look back between consecutive lines about three times as often | measured |
| Weber & Mejia-Ramos 2013, Inglis & Alcock 2013a | Dispute over whether mathematicians skim a proof first | contested |
| Weber 2008 | 8 mathematicians validating proofs use formal, informal and example-based reasoning | measured, qualitative |
| Weber & Mejia-Ramos 2011 | 9 mathematicians read by authority, line by line, or by modules | measured, qualitative |
| Mejia-Ramos & Weber 2014 | Survey of 118 mathematicians on why and how they read proofs (secondary count) | measured, survey |
| Inglis 2013b | 109 mathematicians disagreed on whether one calculus proof was valid | measured |
| Fuller 2014 | Structured versus linear proof: structured readers better at picking a summary, somewhat worse on justifications, transfer and examples, mostly not significant | measured, one study |
| Roy 2017b | e-Proof (N = 21) versus textbook proof (N = 28): equal at once, e-Proof group retained less after two weeks; sample sizes not checked against the paper | measured |
| Lew 2020 | Generic versus traditional proof, randomised: no difference in comprehension, though students preferred generic | measured |
| Hodds 2014 | Self-explanation training before reading: d = 0.95 in the lab; held in a classroom study (Alcock 2015; the count of 107 students not checked) | measured |
| Davies 2020 | Proof summaries scored by comparative judgement are reliable; top summaries name the logical structure | measured |
| Weber 2015 | Strategies of good student readers: try the proof first, find the framework, split into parts, test with examples (secondary) | supported with caveat |

## 6. Formal proofs as explicit structure (pointer only)

- mathlib 2020: a library of formal mathematics in Lean, where every dependency is checked by machine.
- leanblueprint: a LaTeX outline in which each statement lists the labels it uses, from which a dependency graph is drawn and coloured by formal status.
- Gowers 2023 and PFR: the Polynomial Freiman-Ruzsa proof was formalised through such a blueprint within weeks (Tao 2023).
- Tao 2023: formalising surfaced missing hypotheses in the written proof, such as a set required to be nonempty.
