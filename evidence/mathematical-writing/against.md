# Against: the evidence against, the controversies, the failed tests

## 1. Structured proofs do not always improve comprehension

| Evidence | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Structured readers were better only at picking a summary; on justifications, transfer and examples they did somewhat worse, mostly not significantly | Fuller 2014 | measured, one study |
| In interviews, many students said structured proofs "jumped around" and forced them to scan for related parts | Fuller 2014 | measured, qualitative |
| Lamport reports mixed reactions: checkers liked the format, readers who wanted to skim did not | Lamport 1995 | supported with caveat (author's report) |
| Lamport concedes the format does not serve a reader who wants the ideas without the details | Lamport 1995 | supported with caveat |
| A referee found Lamport's long structured proofs tedious and asked for informal sketches of the key ideas | Lamport 2012 | supported with caveat (one referee) |
| Leron's format, in Lamport's view, is no better than prose at avoiding errors | Lamport 1995 | convention, untested |

The only randomised test of a structured layout is Fuller 2014, and it tested Leron's format, not Lamport's.

## 2. More support in the text can backfire

| Evidence | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Narrated, animated e-Proofs gave no immediate gain and lower retention two weeks later | Roy 2017b | measured |
| Eye tracking suggested e-Proof readers made less effort when the narration stopped | Roy 2017b | measured |
| Students liked e-Proofs and generic proofs; neither raised comprehension | Alcock 2015, Lew 2020 | measured |

Student preference is therefore not evidence that a format works.

## 3. The cost of formality

| Cost | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Structured proofs are longer, mainly because they carry more detail | Lamport 1995 | supported with caveat |
| Machine-checked proofs are, for now, much harder to write than careful informal ones | Lamport 2012 | supported with caveat |
| To an expert the formal setup is often redundant: the reader reconstructs the idea and skips the rest | Thurston 1994 | supported with caveat |
| Formal correctness at the scale of whole fields is impractical, and reliability comes from people thinking carefully | Thurston 1994 | supported with caveat |
| Proofs are accepted through a social process that formal verification does not reproduce | De Millo 1979 | convention, untested |
| The school two-column format made proof a routine and cost students' part in finding the ideas | Herbst 2002 | supported with caveat (historical analysis) |
| One of Halmos's advisers found long chains of elaborately stated lemmas boring | Halmos 1970 | convention, untested |

## 4. The DTP style as hiding intuition

- Thurston 1994 argues the DTP picture leaves out where questions come from and how understanding moves between people.
- Thurston 1994 observes that communication narrows from conversation to talk to paper, and the paper keeps the least.
- Hanna 1990 asks for proofs that explain, not only proofs that prove.
- Weber 2004 documents a real-analysis course taught as a sequence of definitions, theorems and proofs (secondary).
- Lamport 2012 replies that prose proofs do not explain either, and that a sketch placed before each level explains better than prose woven into the proof.

## 5. Where the field disagrees

| Disagreement | Sides | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| What a proof is for | Leron 1983: communication; Lamport 1995: rigor; Lamport 2012: separate the two goals | convention, untested |
| Whether a given proof is valid | 109 mathematicians split on one calculus proof; applied mathematicians accepted it more often | measured (Inglis 2013b) |
| Whether experts skim first | Inglis & Alcock 2012 found no initial skim; Weber & Mejia-Ramos 2013 reanalysed and found skimming; Inglis & Alcock 2013a replied | contested |
| Whether series of lemmas help | Halmos's advisers contradicted each other | convention, untested |
| Where notation goes | Introduction, separate section, or along the way; no standard place in 22 articles | measured, descriptive (Kuteeva & McGrath 2015) |
| How an article ends | 11 of 22 have no conclusion; some end with QED, some with open questions | measured, descriptive (Kuteeva & McGrath 2015) |
| Labelling speculation | Jaffe & Quinn 1993 proposed it; Thurston 1994 answered that it misdescribes how mathematics progresses | convention, untested |

## 6. Limits of the evidence itself

- Every comprehension experiment found uses undergraduates, not research mathematicians reading papers.
- Samples are small: Roy 2017b had 49 students; Fuller 2014's size was not read.
- Fuller 2014 has no replication that we found.
- The self-explanation studies (Hodds 2014) come from one team, though across lab and classroom.
- The genre corpora (Graves 2013, Kuteeva & McGrath 2015) are 30 and 22 articles.
- Krantz 2017 says his article scheme may not fit papers that fill a whole journal issue, such as the Feit-Thompson or Wiles papers.
