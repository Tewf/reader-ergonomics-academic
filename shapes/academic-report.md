# Shape: academic report

The shape of a report written for a course, an internship, a master's degree or a lab. It is one skeleton for four
kinds, each part marked with the kinds that need it, and every heading is in French and in English.

Its body is the [report shape of reader-ergonomics](https://github.com/Tewf/reader-ergonomics/blob/v1.1.1/shapes/report.md), a solved
problem written up step by step. This shape puts that body inside the frame an institution asks for: a title page, an
abstract, the context, the references. The evidence is the [reports review](../evidence/reports/), and its baseline is
the technical-report standard NISO 2010.

## When to use it

| Code | Kind | Read by |
|---|---|---|
| PR | course project report, rapport de projet | the teacher who grades it |
| IR | internship report, rapport de stage | the school, and the host organisation |
| MT | master's thesis, [mémoire de master](../evidence/reports/frame.md) | a jury, then a defence |
| TR | technical report, rapport technique | a lab, a company, readers of a preprint |

- Not for an article sent to a journal: use the [research paper](research-paper.md) shape.
- Not for a single long proof: use the [proof](proof.md) shape.
- When the course or the school prescribes its own headings, keep them and their order, and map each one to a part
  below. No study says otherwise, so the school's rule wins. Parts the school does not name, such as the
  introduction or the conclusion, go before and after its headings, never inside them.
- The defence (soutenance) is a talk, and belongs to
  [reader-ergonomics-presentations](https://github.com/Tewf/reader-ergonomics-presentations).

## How each part maps to the evidence

| Part | What it does | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title page | names the report by its finding, then the school's items: school, supervisor, host, jury, date | NISO 2010; EOST 2026 (example) | convention, untested |
| Abstract | question, method, result and limit, in about 200 words, in both languages when the school asks | NISO 2010; most examiners open the abstract, introduction and conclusion first (Mullins 2002) | convention; supported with caveat for the early reading |
| Executive summary | the answer and what to do, for a reader who reads nothing else | NISO 2010; a preference test (Silyn-Roberts 2002); a null pilot of a methods summary (Koetsenruijter 2022) | supported with caveat: no controlled test on reports |
| Introduction | the question, its scope and the plan; in a thesis, the contribution in one sentence | NISO 2010; examiners form a first impression early, usually by the end of chapter 2 or 3 (Mullins 2002) | supported with caveat: interviews with 30 examiners |
| Host organisation | one page of context, the rest in an appendix | TSE 2017 (example); UT 2024 | convention, untested |
| Mission and objectives | the objective and its success test before the method; for an internship, the learning objectives too | TSE 2017 (example); UT 2024 | convention, untested |
| Literature and background | for a thesis, a chapter that opens with its answer and ends each section on what it implies for the question | Holbrook 2007; the first impression often forms at the literature review (Mullins 2002) | supported with caveat |
| Method | the approach, why this one, what it cannot show, each step named by what it achieves | NISO 2010; examiners ask for a justified approach with its limits (Golding 2014); the step names come from the standard's worked examples | supported with caveat |
| Results | each figure beside the text that explains it | NISO 2010 | convention for reports; the figure placement is measured in reader-ergonomics |
| Discussion | the findings interpreted and tied to the literature, with no claim beyond the data | Golding 2014; NISO 2010 | supported with caveat |
| Reflection | for an internship, what was learnt against the stated objectives | UT 2024; UGA 2026 | convention, untested |
| Conclusion | repeats the answer and stands alone, with nothing new | NISO 2010 asks that it be read alone; readers say they read it (Pinelli 1982, self-report survey); examiners open it first (Mullins 2002) | supported with caveat: no controlled test of conclusion first |
| Recommendations | only when the report proposes action; in a thesis, the purpose restated and consolidated | NISO 2010; Bunton 2005 | convention, untested |
| Contributions | who did and wrote what, in a report by a team | the school's or the course's rule | convention, untested |
| Use of AI tools | whether and how AI tools were used, in the words the school asks for; for a journal, the research paper shape's declaration | the school's or the course's rule | convention, untested |
| Headings | chapter labels stay conventional; headings below them state the claim | Lorch 1996 (free recall, unfamiliar topics) | measured in that setting; untested for reports |
| Language and presentation | form corrected by the checks, not tuned past them | presentation errors make examiners read more critically (Golding 2014); graders agree poorly on form (Brown 2023, in its first round; Bodnar 2018) | supported with caveat |
| Length | the school's limit, stated on the title page; in a thesis, each chapter opens with a recap and ends with a bridge, since it is read in sittings | EOST 2026 (example); examiners spend three or four days on a thesis, spread over two to three weeks (Mullins 2002) | convention, untested |

Each key (Author Year) is listed in the [bibliography](../evidence/bibliography.md). The reading order and the timing of examiners come from Mullins 2002, read in full: interviews with 30 experienced
examiners. Golding 2014, a review of 30 studies of thesis examination, also read in full, adds that the first impression
changes only slowly. It cites no study for that statement, so it is marked as the review's own.

## Where academic convention and the standard disagree

| Conflict | What wins | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A story told in time order against the result first | the abstract, the summary and the conclusion carry the result first and stand alone; the body may follow time when time is the object, as in an internship | readers and examiners look at the summary and the conclusion first (Pinelli 1982, Mullins 2002); tests of the order of a whole text are mixed (Spyridakis 2000, Spyridakis 2002, Jansen 2010) |
| No findings in the introduction against an early verdict | in a thesis, the introduction ends with the contribution in one sentence; the result itself stays in the abstract | examiners form a first impression early, usually by the end of chapter 2 or 3 and often at the literature review (Mullins 2002), and it changes only slowly, a review's statement with no study cited (Golding 2014) |
| A long literature chapter against using each idea where it is needed | a chapter for the thesis, which examiners expect; folded into the introduction for project and technical reports; none for an internship | Holbrook 2007; the internship forms grade no literature (UT 2024) |
| Thanks at the front against the answer first | acknowledgements at the back | NISO 2010 makes them optional |
| Topic labels against sentence headings | labels for chapters, claims below them | Lorch 1996 |
| Impersonal against active voice, and length | the school's rule | untested either way; surveyed NASA readers preferred the third person (Pinelli 1984), a stated preference, not a test |

## Skeleton

Copy this into a new file, or start from the LaTeX version, [academic-report.tex](academic-report.tex). Each part says
which kinds need it; delete the parts your kind leaves out.

````markdown
# Title that states the finding

Author, school and degree, course or host organisation, supervisor, date. The school's length limit, if it sets one.

## Résumé · Abstract

> All kinds but PR, where it is optional. The question, the method, the result and its limit, in about 200 words.
> The same text in French and in English when the school asks, with at most five keywords.

## Synthèse · Executive summary

> MT and TR; optional for PR and IR. The answer and what to do, for a reader who reads nothing else, in one page.

## Sommaire · Contents

> MT and TR; PR and IR past 20 pages.

## 1. Introduction

> All kinds. The question, its scope and the plan of the report. MT: end with the contribution in one sentence.
> PR and TR: the background the question needs, briefly, here rather than in a chapter.

## 2. Contexte · Host organisation

> IR only. One page: what the organisation does, and where the internship sat in it. The rest goes to an appendix.

## 3. Mission et objectifs · Mission and objectives

> PR and IR. The objective and the test that says it is met; IR: the learning objectives too.

## 4. État de l'art · Literature and background

> MT only, as a chapter. Open with a one-paragraph answer; end each section on what it implies for the question.

## 5. Méthode · Method

> All kinds. The approach, why this one, and what it cannot show. One step per subsection, named by what it achieves,
> the figure of the process first.

## 6. Résultats · Results

> All kinds. One subsection per result, its headline sentence first, the figure beside the text that explains it.

## 7. Discussion

> All kinds. What the results mean, how they compare with the literature, and their limits; no claim beyond the data.

## 8. Retour réflexif · Reflection

> IR only. What was learnt, against the learning objectives stated in section 3.

## 9. Conclusion

> All kinds. The answer again, standing alone: a reader who opens the report here understands it. Nothing new.

## 10. Recommandations et perspectives · Recommendations and perspectives

> IR, MT and TR, only when the report proposes action. MT: the purpose restated and the contribution consolidated.

## Contributions

> PR and TR written by more than one author. Who did and wrote what.

## Déclaration d'usage de l'IA · Use of AI tools

> All kinds, when the school or the course asks. Whether and how AI tools were used, in its words.

## Remerciements · Acknowledgements

> Optional, IR and MT, at the back so that the answer comes first.

## Glossaire · Glossary

> MT and TR. Every term defined at its first use, and listed here.

## Bibliographie · References

> All kinds, in the school's style; every entry with a DOI or a stable URL.

## Annexes · Appendices

> Detail a reader can skip: the host organisation's longer presentation, raw data, code listings.
````

In a thesis, open each chapter with two lines recalling where the argument stands, and end it with one line leading
to the next: an examiner reads it over several days (Mullins 2002).
